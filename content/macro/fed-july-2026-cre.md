## The setup

The Federal Open Market Committee meets Tuesday and Wednesday, July 28 and 29. The target range for the federal funds rate currently sits at 3.50 to 3.75 percent, unchanged since the June meeting, where the Committee voted unanimously to hold. There is no Summary of Economic Projections attached to this meeting, which means no updated dot plot and no fresh set of official forecasts to trade against.

What makes this particular meeting interesting is not the likely outcome. Most of the market expects a hold, and a hold is what the consensus of sell-side previews calls for. What makes it interesting is that the range of respectable opinion has widened considerably, and the Chair has stopped narrowing it for us.

Kevin Warsh, sworn in as Chair in May, has largely abandoned the practice of forward guidance that defined the Powell and Yellen eras. The Committee is no longer telling markets in advance what it intends to do. That is a defensible philosophical position, and Warsh held it long before he had the job, but it has a practical consequence: uncertainty that used to be resolved by a speech now has to be resolved by the meeting itself. Volatility that once clustered around guidance now clusters around outcomes.

## The genuine disagreement

The June dot plot showed nine of eighteen policymakers expecting at least one rate hike in 2026. Three months earlier, not a single participant had penciled one in. That is a fast move, and it reflects a committee that has watched inflation run above target for the better part of five years and is starting to worry openly about the credibility cost of tolerating it.

Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan has been the most direct about it, arguing publicly that a moderate increase would be appropriate. The hawkish case is straightforward. Inflation has been sticky, oil has spiked on renewed geopolitical tension, and a central bank that has missed its target for five consecutive years has a reputational problem that compounds the longer it goes unaddressed.

The case for holding is equally straightforward and probably stronger on the data. June core CPI came in softer than expected, PPI cooperated, and payroll growth has slowed. Citi's preview argues markets are overstating the odds of a near-term hike precisely because the recent prints undercut the justification for one. If the Committee did not hike in June, when the inflation data looked worse, it is difficult to explain hiking in July when it looks better.

Market pricing has been unstable, which is itself the story. Depending on the day and the source, the implied probability of no change has run anywhere from the mid-sixties to the high nineties, with roughly 30 percent priced for a hike after the recent move in crude. Odds of a September hike have climbed materially. The market is not confused about direction so much as about timing.

## Why this matters less to real estate than people think

Here is the part that gets lost in the coverage. The federal funds rate is an overnight rate. Almost nothing in commercial real estate prices off an overnight rate directly. Fixed-rate permanent debt prices off the ten-year Treasury plus a spread. Floating-rate bridge and construction debt prices off SOFR plus a spread. SOFR tracks the policy rate closely, so the front end matters for anyone carrying floating paper, but the valuation math that determines what a stabilized asset is worth runs through the long end.

That distinction is why the Fed decision is often the least important thing that happens on Fed day. What moves the ten-year is the statement language, the press conference, and the market's revised read on the path over the next twelve months. A hold accompanied by hawkish language can push the ten-year higher and hurt values more than a hike accompanied by language suggesting it is the last one.

The ten-year has already repriced meaningfully. Thirty-year fixed mortgage rates were at 6.85 percent on July 22, near their highest level since November 2025, which tells you the long end has taken the June turn seriously. For CRE, the transmission runs like this: higher ten-year, wider all-in borrowing cost, lower proceeds at a given debt service coverage constraint, more equity required, lower price the buyer can pay to hit the same levered return. Cap rates do not have to move for values to fall. The debt constraint does the work.

## The cap rate spread question

Multifamily cap rates have been grinding sideways rather than expanding further, which most of the research shops read as a peak. The spread between cap rates and the ten-year is the thing to watch. Historically that spread averages somewhere in the range of 150 to 250 basis points for core assets. When it compresses below 100 you are looking at a market pricing perfection. When it blows out past 300 you are looking at either genuine distress or an unusually good entry point.

Right now the spread is thin by historical standards, which is the uncomfortable part of the current setup. Buyers are not being paid much of a risk premium over the risk-free rate. That works if the ten-year falls from here and cap rates compress along with it, which is the bull case most of the 2026 forecasts were built on. It works considerably less well if the Fed is actually done cutting and the long end stays where it is or drifts higher on term premium and heavy Treasury issuance.

For a buyer underwriting today, that argues for two things. First, do not underwrite exit cap compression. If your returns require the exit cap to be tighter than the going-in cap, you are underwriting a macro view rather than a business plan. Second, be honest about where the return actually comes from. In an environment where financial engineering is not doing the work, the return has to come from NOI growth, which means rent growth, expense control, and operational execution.

## What to watch Wednesday

The vote itself matters less than the dissents. A unanimous hold reads very differently from a hold with two or three dissents in favor of a hike, because the second version tells you September is live in a way the market has not fully absorbed. Watch the statement's characterization of inflation risk versus employment risk, since both Vice Chairs have recently framed the balance as tilted toward inflation. And watch the ten-year in the hour after the press conference rather than the minute after the statement, because the press conference is where an unguided Chair does the most damage or the most good.

For those of us underwriting deals, the practical takeaway is narrow. Nothing that happens Wednesday is likely to change a going-in cap rate. What it can change is the forward curve, and therefore the rate cap cost on a floating-rate bridge deal and the assumed refinance rate in year three of a hold. Those are the two line items in a model that are quietly most sensitive to Fed communication, and they are the two that tend to get updated last.

The next meeting with a Summary of Economic Projections and a press conference is September 16 and 17. That is the earliest realistic point at which the picture gets meaningfully clearer.
