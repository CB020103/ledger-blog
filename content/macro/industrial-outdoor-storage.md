I put on a CBRE podcast expecting background noise and came away with a new rabbit hole: industrial outdoor storage, or IOS. It is the least glamorous corner of industrial real estate. Truck yards, container storage, equipment staging, contractor lots, the fenced gravel you drive past without a second thought. The pitch was that this boring gravel has quietly become one of the tightest supply and demand stories in commercial real estate. So I dug in, and the pitch mostly holds up. What surprised me is how fast the institutional capital moved, and what that says about where the opportunity actually sits now.

## The supply is capped by a zoning problem

The whole thesis starts with supply, and supply is basically frozen. IOS sites need to be zoned for outdoor storage, and cities do not want to approve more of them. A truck yard generates a fraction of the tax revenue of a warehouse or a retail center on the same land, brings traffic and noise, and gives a municipality very little to brag about. So most towns quietly resist new IOS zoning, and some have gone further. Southern California's Inland Empire passed AB 98 in 2024, forcing new warehouse and truck route alignment and triggering development moratoriums in several cities.

The kicker is that you cannot build your way around it. To create a new IOS site in an infill location, you often have to buy land with a building already on it and demolish the building just to get usable ground, which almost never pencils. So the existing supply is close to fixed, and every new logistics or infrastructure tenant is fighting over the same lots. That is the supply cliff people in the space keep pointing to.

## The demand keeps showing up

On the other side, the tenants are exactly the businesses that have been growing: logistics and last mile operators, construction and infrastructure firms, equipment rental companies, utilities. Names on recent institutional portfolios include United Rentals, ABC Supply, SiteOne, AT&T, and Linde. These are not tenants who churn. Moving a truck yard or a staging operation is expensive and disruptive, so they tend to renew rather than chase a slightly cheaper lot down the road. That stickiness is a big part of why lenders and buyers like the cash flows.

The land over building model also flips the usual industrial math. You are underwriting dirt and location, not an expensive structure you have to maintain and re-tenant. Lower capital intensity, higher yield than a comparable warehouse, and rent growth tied to logistics and construction demand. On paper it is a clean story.

## The twist: it already went institutional

Here is where my original take needed updating. I assumed IOS was still mostly a fragmented, mom and pop world waiting for public REITs to roll it up and bring scale. The fragmentation part is true. By some estimates the average owner controls a rounding error of the inventory in their own market, and the total U.S. IOS market sits somewhere around $200 to $300 billion depending on who is counting. But the institutional money is not waiting. It already arrived.

Alterra IOS, the largest owner in the space, closed a $925 million fund in 2024 and has bought hundreds of sites. Zenith scaled a joint venture with J.P. Morgan past $1.5 billion. Catalyst raised a $400 million fund that blew past its target and kept buying through 2026, backed by the likes of Blackstone and J.P. Morgan Asset Management. Life insurers and CMBS desks built underwriting frameworks for the asset class. It went from "never heard of it" to a named allocation category in a few years.

The public market angle, the part I was most curious about, basically opened and shut. Peakstone Realty Trust sold off all of its office assets at the end of 2025 to become a pure play industrial and IOS REIT, briefly making it the cleanest public way to own the theme. Then in February 2026 Brookfield agreed to take it private for $1.2 billion, a 34 percent premium, in what became the largest pure IOS deal on record. So the one public vehicle got scooped up by private capital almost as soon as it existed.

## Where I land

The supply and demand story that hooked me is real, and it is durable, because the constraint is zoning and zoning does not loosen quickly. But the easy version of the trade, get in before the institutions, is already gone. The interesting question now is whether IOS stays a private, fund owned asset class that gets absorbed into giants like Brookfield, or whether the scale and cash flow stability eventually pull a new pure play REIT back onto the public market for people who want the exposure without writing a fund commitment. Given how quickly Peakstone got taken out, I would bet the smart money wants to keep this one private for a while.
