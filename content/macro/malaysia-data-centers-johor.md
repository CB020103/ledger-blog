## A constraint is a location decision

The cleanest way to understand the Malaysian data center boom is to stop thinking about Malaysia for a moment and look at Singapore. Singapore has the demand. It has the financial sector, the regional headquarters, the subsea cable landings, the latency advantage, and the legal system that makes multinationals comfortable putting workloads there. What it does not have is land, power, or political tolerance for consuming large amounts of either.

In roughly 2019 Singapore stopped approving new data center projects. There was no formal legislation announcing it. Operators simply found that permits had become impossible to obtain, and the pause was not really acknowledged publicly until late 2020. It ran until 2022, when Singapore reopened the door selectively, allocating only a small initial tranche of new capacity, on the order of 80 megawatts, and attaching sustainability requirements strict enough to raise both construction and operating costs materially. Facilities have to hit power usage effectiveness of 1.3 or better. The result is that Singapore now has some of the most efficient data centers in the world and very few new ones.

Demand did not disappear when supply was capped. It moved twenty miles north across the Johor Strait.

## What Johor captured

Johor was the overwhelming beneficiary of that displacement, absorbing roughly 35 billion dollars in investment. Amazon and Microsoft went in, as did Tencent and Alibaba. By Knight Frank's count, Johor holds around 80 percent of Malaysia's IT capacity, and the character of that capacity is different from what came before. The Klang Valley, encompassing Kuala Lumpur and Cyberjaya, has historically hosted more facilities but smaller ones, mostly under 20 megawatts, serving enterprise, banking, and government workloads. Most of Johor's facilities exceed 100 megawatts. Those are AI training campuses, not colocation halls.

The scale of the pipeline is the number worth sitting with. By mid-2025, planned capacity under construction or approved in Johor had reached approximately 5.7 gigawatts, against a Singapore pipeline of roughly 1.6 gigawatts. JLL's figure for Johor's planned and under-construction capacity implies an eightfold increase from current operational levels to around 7,000 megawatts. Operational capacity today is a much smaller fraction of that, somewhere in the range of one to two gigawatts, which is the gap that matters when assessing how much of this actually gets built.

The economics behind the move were not subtle. Before Malaysia's tariff restructuring in July 2025, industrial power ran around ten cents per kilowatt hour against roughly twenty-seven cents in Singapore. Land was cheaper and available. The Johor-Singapore Special Economic Zone added preferential treatment for operators, streamlined cross-border movement, and integrated power and connectivity planning across the border. Tenaga Nasional's grid connection fast-track program compressed timelines that are usually the binding constraint on a build. Malaysia stacked a cost advantage on top of a proximity advantage on top of a policy advantage.

## The part that is changing

Here is where it gets genuinely interesting, and where the received narrative is now a year out of date.

Malaysia is starting to do what Singapore did. Johor announced a blanket ban on two categories of extremely water-intensive facilities, the kind consuming as much as 50 million liters per day. New projects have to demonstrate how they will source electricity, with renewable supply increasingly a precondition for approval. Selangor is vetting rather than banning, but is requiring 30 percent local content in areas like integrated circuit design and cooling systems, on the explicit reasoning that these projects have historically delivered limited economic spillover to the communities hosting them.

The politics have arrived as well. Reuters reported protests over a data center complex at Johor's southern tip, the first of their kind in the country, and the debate in Selangor has become politicized. In April the opposition Socialist Party publicly attacked a hyperscale project worth 1.75 billion ringgit, roughly 428 million dollars, as prioritizing corporations over communities. Malaysia's July 2025 tariff restructuring also narrowed the power cost gap that anchored the original pitch.

None of this stops the boom. Analysts describe a second wave already breaking ground beyond Johor, at Springhill in Negeri Sembilan and Serendah in Selangor, and the IMF has cited data center activity as a meaningful support for Malaysia's 2026 growth. But Savills is already seeing inbound requests shift toward Thailand, where larger campuses are being built in the south. The pattern that sent demand from Singapore to Johor is beginning to send it from Johor onward.

## Why a US real estate person should care

I do not underwrite Malaysian industrial land, and neither does most of my likely audience. The reason this is worth following is that it is the clearest available case study in something that governs domestic markets too.

Data center siting is not a demand story. It is a constraint story. Demand for compute is close to unlimited at current prices and is not the variable that determines where a campus lands. Power availability, water availability, grid interconnection queue position, and local political tolerance determine where a campus lands. When any one of those binds, the demand does not evaporate. It relocates to the nearest place where the constraint does not bind, and it usually relocates faster than the receiving market's infrastructure and politics can absorb it.

That is exactly the dynamic playing out in Northern Virginia, in central Ohio, in Phoenix, and increasingly in the Southeast. Loudoun County ran into the same wall Singapore ran into, and the same overflow reached the same kind of adjacent market. The Johor sequence, from open arms to selective approval to categorical bans on the most resource-intensive uses, took about six years. That is a reasonable prior for how long a US market has between welcoming these projects and starting to constrain them.

For anyone underwriting land in the path of that growth, the lesson cuts both ways. Being early in an overflow market is where the returns are, because you are buying before the constraint prices in. But the same political sequence that made your market attractive is running in your market too, on a delay. The exit assumption should account for the possibility that by the time you sell, the buyer is underwriting a jurisdiction that has started saying no.

The second-order real estate effects are the ones nobody models well. A 100-megawatt campus does not employ many people once it is running, which is precisely the complaint Selangor's officials are making. Construction employment is real but temporary. The durable multifamily and retail demand generated per dollar of capital deployed is far lower than for almost any other industrial use. That is worth remembering the next time a data center announcement gets cited as a demand driver in a market study.
